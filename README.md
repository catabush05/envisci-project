# Rising Temperatures and Native Tree Resilience in New England
Contributors: Pen Hanks, Maddie Strate, Catalina Bush

## Abstract
New England has some of the fastest rising temperatures in the world, and native tree populations are suffering because of it. This project investigates how temperature and other climatic variables impact tree presence across New England as well as tree mortality in Harvard Forest. We use a combination of predictive models (zero-inflated negative binomial, random forest, etc.) and causal analysis to measure tree resilience and habitat preference by species and location. These findings will help inform future reforestation and conservation efforts amid a warming climate.

## Executive Summary

**Background**

The New England region, which includes Vermont, New Hampshire, Maine, Massachusetts, Connecticut, Rhode Island, and northern New York, is witnessing historic warming levels that have resulted in the region already surpassing benchmarks set by third party agencies. The effects of climate change also extend beyond temperature increases, with precipitation and seasonal patterns fluctuating wildly.

**Problem Summary**

Research shows that the New England region could see temperature increases of up to 7.6℉, with widespread ramifications resulting from this change. The skiing industry — which has long been an economic driver in many parts of New England — is no longer as stable as it used to be, with decreased snowfall and warmer winters shortening skiing seasons. Increased prevalence of droughts, extreme heat days, and high precipitation days are also being caused by climate change, creating a more volatile environment that is less conducive to tree growth. Additionally, this extreme weather can encourage the presence of harmful pests that feed on and kill tree species, as well as increase the prevalence of existing diseases which further endanger native tree species. 

**Solution Summary**

Two solutions are outlined, both of which impact key stakeholders and are able to be feasibly completed within a reasonable timeframe. The first solution involves pre-commercial thinning, a strategy that can help mitigate infections and harmful pests in forests by selectively thinning trees and artificially selecting for the strongest specimens. This strategy has been used in the past, and could be adapted to address issues in the New England region. The second solution is to assess a wide variety of tree species that can be used as new foundation species for adaptive forests. The results of research contained within this study highlight several key findings that must be accounted for when performing this kind of assessment. Primarily, studies that test the adaptivity of tree species must be conducted in real-world environments, as controlled studies introduce confounding variables that muddle results. 

**Recommendations**

Our primary recommendation for NEFF is to increase tree thinning in the 40,000 acres of land that they own. Thinning in areas with eastern hemlock and American beech trees will play a part in mitigating the spread of beech leaf disease and the hemlock woolly adelgid, two major threats to Northeastern forests. This strategy will encourage healthier trees, provide valuable data for addressing future threats, and buy time for more research into replacing declining forests.
